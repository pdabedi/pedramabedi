Research

I work with Dr. Keren Sharon at the University of Michigan, developing strong gravitational lens models of galaxy clusters. My research supports investigations of dark matter, galaxy evolution, and the expansion of the Universe.

Strong Gravitational Lens Modeling

Massive galaxy clusters bend the light from background galaxies, producing magnified, distorted, and sometimes multiple images of the same source. I use JWST and archival Hubble Space Telescope imaging, together with available spectroscopy, to identify these images and match features between them. Their positions and redshifts constrain models of the clusters’ total mass distributions, including dark matter. JWST reveals additional galaxies and fine structure within their lensed images, providing a dense set of constraints on the mass models.

I also develop Python tools to analyze these models, measure magnifications and enclosed masses, and reconstruct background galaxies before their light was distorted by lensing. An important part of my work is evaluating how observational uncertainties and modeling assumptions affect these measurements. The resulting models and analysis products support the projects below.

Current Work

JWST LEGGOS: Resolving Galaxy Evolution

The LEGGOS survey (LEnsing and Galaxy Growth: Observing Substructures) combines gravitational magnification with JWST imaging and spectroscopy to investigate star formation and quenching within distant galaxies. These observations reveal individual stellar structures near the peak of cosmic star formation, allowing the collaboration to study how different regions grow and evolve within a galaxy.

I contribute lens models, magnification measurements, source-plane reconstructions, and flux measurements. These products allow collaborators to translate observed sizes and brightnesses into intrinsic physical properties, providing the foundation for interpreting the galaxies’ stellar populations and star-forming regions.

JWST SLICE: Mapping Galaxy Cluster Evolution

The SLICE survey (Strong LensIng and Cluster Evolution) investigates how dark matter and ordinary matter assemble within massive galaxy clusters across approximately eight billion years of cosmic history, spanning redshifts of z ≈ 0.2–1.9. I contribute strong-lens models and associated analysis products that map the clusters’ mass distributions. Comparing these maps with the distribution of galaxies and diffuse stellar light helps the collaboration investigate how clusters build their stellar content and redistribute matter as they evolve.

Time-Delay Cosmography: Lensed Quasars and Supernovae

Gravitational lensing also provides a way to measure the expansion of the Universe. When a quasar varies in brightness or a supernova explodes, its multiple images show the event at different times because the light follows different paths through the cluster’s gravitational field. Combining measured time delays with a lens model allows us to constrain the Hubble constant, H0, which describes the present-day expansion rate. This approach is known as time-delay cosmography.

I construct cluster lens models for studies of wide-separation lensed quasars, including systems from COOL-LAMPS. I am also a co-author of the discovery study of SN Helios, a strongly lensed Type II supernova at z = 3.34. Lens models predict a future appearance of another image of this supernova, offering an opportunity to extend time-delay cosmography to a source beyond redshift three.

Notable Results

A Strong Lens Model of SDSS J1110+6459

As part of LEGGOS, I developed a strong lens model of SDSS J1110+6459, a galaxy cluster at z = 0.659 that lenses the star-forming galaxy SGAS J1110 at z = 2.481 into three images forming a giant arc.

Using JWST NIRCam imaging, NIRSpec spectroscopy, and archival Hubble observations, we identified four multiply imaged background sources and matched more than 20 luminous regions across the primary arc.

The resulting model reproduces the observed image positions with an image-plane RMS of 0.11 arcseconds and gives a total effective areal magnification of approximately 24.2 for the giant arc. The dense set of resolved clump constraints improves magnification precision by approximately 2.6–8.3 times over previous models. We also estimate a systematic uncertainty of approximately 7%.

Tracing the three lensed images back through the model produces consistent source-plane reconstructions of the background galaxy. This lensing solution provides the foundation for subsequent studies of its star-forming structures, allowing observed sizes and brightnesses to be translated into intrinsic physical properties.
